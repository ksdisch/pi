# Constellation co-op pilot, run 5 — planet-1, 2026-08-13

Two runs, back to back, against constellation `604a422` — the first co-op play
since the jump-outcome telemetry (PR #25) landed, and the first under the
arm-ordering protocol that pilot 4's finding 4 asked for (folded into the seat
prompts on this branch: the laptop's ask and armed `/move` go out in the same
turn, and the phone holds a freeze cast until its partner says they are
arming). Seats: laptop `google/gemini-3.5-flash-lite`, phone
`google/gemini-3.1-flash-lite`.

Run A `20260813-221712`: ~3m27s of play, 11 deaths, no clear.
Run B `20260813-223147`: ~1m54s of play, 5 deaths, no clear.
Both laptop seats were killed by the token-rate 429
(`GenerateContentInputTokensPerModelPerMinute-FreeTier`, 250k input
tokens/min), with `Please retry in 14.2s` and `11.6s` respectively. Times below
are UTC transcript stamps, following pilot 4's convention: driver observations
are tool-reply stamps, seat actions are tool-call stamps.

**The question this pilot exists to answer: do the new `jump` and
`lastStoodAt` fields change what a seat can reason about at the pit?** Pilot 4
ended on exactly this gap — two different jump failures produced identical
replies, so "the pair never tested the jump" — and the aim sweep then settled
the aim question offline. This is the first time live seats played with the
answer available to them.

**Yes — and the run produced the harness's first seat-issued jump verdict.**
Run A's laptop reasoned its way from `lastStoodAt` pit data to a plan it
announced as "I will jump across the pit at x=630", issued
`{jumpAtX: 630, untilX: 850}`, and got back `jump: {tookOff: true, pressedAt:
{x: 632, y: 476}, apexY: 363}` — a real take-off, verdict attached, from a
seat that could finally tell it happened. Pilot 4's central ambiguity (an
attempted jump indistinguishable from no jump) did not reproduce. What the
jump also produced is this pilot's sharpest new finding: the aim was fine and
the *release* was wrong — `untilX: 850` never cut input inside the flight, so
the astronaut overflew the whole bridge and came down past its far end, still
over the pit — and that death is invisible to the death telemetry, because it
completed after the move had already returned (see "Deaths the driver cannot
see" below).

Runs 1–4 are `PILOT-2026-08-11.md`, `PILOT-2026-08-11-run2.md`,
`PILOT-2026-08-12.md`, and `PILOT-2026-08-13.md`.

## The jump — pilot 4's owed experiment, run live

The sequence, all driver-logged fields, run A:

- 03:20:38.301 the laptop announces: "Need one more Freeze Stars to reach
  x=564, then I will jump across the pit at x=630. Cast Freeze Stars, arming
  on freeze." The 630 is reasoned, not guessed: its three pit deaths to that
  point all carried `lastStoodAt` at x=644–648, ground height — the last spot
  it stood before falling — so it aimed just inside the nearest lip it had
  evidence for. (The true fall edge is x≈656; the aim sweep put the reasoned
  number well inside the workable band.)
- 03:20:42.129 the armed approach fires (`armedForMs` 559) and parks at
  x=576, enemy frozen — the two-stage plan run B of pilot 4 invented and
  never completed.
- 03:20:43.237 `{"dir":"right","jumpAtX":630,"untilX":850}`.
- 03:20:44.477 the reply: `events: ["jumped","reached-x"]`,
  `jump: {tookOff: true, pressedAt: {x: 632, y: 476}, apexY: 363}`, ending at
  x=852, y=459 — airborne, descending, past the bridge's far end (the bridge
  spans x≈722–818; past 818 there is still pit below).
- 03:20:45.633 the next `/state`: `respawnCount` 11 — dead — with `lastDeath`
  still the *previous* death (`respawnCount: 10`). The death the seat most
  needed to see has no record anywhere.

Two things are true at once. The telemetry did its job: `tookOff: true` told
the seat the aim was tested (pilot 4's runs never got that far), and
`pressedAt` pinned where. And the failure it revealed is precisely the aim
sweep's headline reproduced from the seat side — nothing released the run.
The sweep's 6-for-6 landings all carried an `untilX` *over the bridge*
(untilX 780); this seat chose 850, past the far gap, so the input never cut
and the arc carried over everything. Whether the astronaut grazed the bridge
on the way through cannot be told from the reply; what is certain is that at
the move's end it was at x=852 still descending, and one second later it was
dead with no site reported.

The laptop was killed by the 429 within seconds of that `/state`, so the run
ends there: one verdict-carrying jump, aimed right, released wrong, its death
unrecorded. The experiment the next session owes is narrower than
pilot 4's: same park, same jump, `untilX` over the bridge instead of past it —
and the seat prompt already carries everything needed to reason that out
*if the seat can see where the overflight died*, which today it cannot.

`lastStoodAt` earned its place: every pit death named the ground at the lip
(644–652, y=476) — the "never reached the bridge" class — and that is the
data run A's laptop turned into its x=630 aim. No bridge-height (429) rest
appears anywhere: no seat ever stood on the bridge in either run.

**The three uncaptured deaths are a class, not an accident.** A `/move`
reply can only report a death that happens *inside* the move. All three
run-A gaps are deaths that completed after the move returned: a move that
ends `reached-x` while mid-fall (twice — including the jump), and a freeze
that lapsed while the astronaut stood parked between calls. In each case the
seat's next `/state` showed `respawnCount` advanced with `lastDeath` still
carrying the *previous* death's `respawnCount` — so the freshness rule
shipped in PR #21 did exactly its job (no seat was misled into quoting a
stale site; the run-A laptop correctly went looking after seeing the count
jump), but nothing supplies the missing site. The jump overflight is the
worst instance, because it is the one death this pilot most existed to
observe. Sixteen deaths across both runs: 13 captured with real sites, 3
invisible — and the invisible ones cluster exactly where the interesting
play is, at the end of moves that reached their target.

## The protocol fix, measured

Pilot 4: three `arm-timeout`s across two runs, two of them ~90-second
double-blocked stalls, 27% of run B's playable life lost to one of them.
Pilot 5, with the finding-4 protocol in the prompts: **fifteen arms across
both runs, fifteen fired, zero timeouts, zero deadlocks.** Every laptop ask
and its armed `/move` went out in the same turn (typically ~1s apart); no
freeze was ever cast against an absent arm. Run B's phone had internalized
the protocol well enough to initiate it — "Ready for either. Just let me know
when you're ARMED and which power you want" (03:32:15.709), before its
partner had asked for anything — and later held with "Understood. Let me know
when you're ARMED and I'll cast freeze" (03:33:07.300).

The freeze-armed rows bear it out: nine freeze arms, `armedForMs` 497–2841,
every one placed before its cast landed and fired into a live window. The
astronaut crossed the patrol band under every one of them.

**What replaced the deadlock is the stale platform.** Five of the fifteen
arms were `arm: {on: "platform"}` placed while a platform from an earlier
attempt was still standing — platforms now persist indefinitely — so the
level-based trigger fired in 60–64ms with no freeze up, and all five ended in
a death (four in the patrol band at x=312–428, one into the pit). Pilot 4
saw this once and correctly called instant-fire the *right* semantics for a
platform arm; what the pattern adds is that after the first platform cast of
a run, "arm on platform" can never again express coordination — the
condition is permanently true, so the arm is just "go now, unprotected", and
seats kept reaching for it as though it still meant "when my partner's cast
lands". Both run-A seats even diagnosed it in the moment ("Old platform
triggered immediate arm and enemy got me at 312") and then reached for it
again two minutes later. This sharpens the backlog's trigger-life item: the
freeze case wants `freezeMsLeft` so an instant fire can be told from a fresh
one, and the platform case wants the equivalent honesty — an `armedForMs` of
~60 on a platform arm means the trigger was already true when you armed.

## The 429 wall moved closer, because the fix worked

Both laptop seats died on the 250k input-tokens/min cap, like both pilot-4
runs — but faster: 3m27s and 1m54s of play against pilot 4's 3.5 and 5.5
minutes. The mechanism is visible in the turn cadence. Run A's laptop made
50 assistant turns in 3m27s (14.5/min; summed `input + cacheRead` 771k);
run B's made 33 in 1m54s (17.4/min; 398k). Pilot 4's laptops turned at
10.4–11.1/min. The protocol fix removed the 90-second stalls that were
pilot 4's only quota relief — its finding 7 noted the deadlocks were "the
only thing that kept either run alive as long as it was" — so a pair that
coordinates *better* now burns its token budget *sooner*. Run B compounds
it: launched one minute after run A's orchestrator ended, it also started
closer to a warm quota window and played the briskest of the four
pilot-4/5 runs. The free-tier wall is now unambiguously the binding
constraint on live pilots: the backlog's token-rate-429 retryability item
(or paid-tier seats) is what buys the next pilot more than ~4 minutes.

Run B's phone deserves its own line: after its partner went silent at
~03:33, it cast one unrequested Illuminate (its prompt forbids unasked
casts), then sat through **five** consecutive 300s `intercom_wait`s — its
own stop rule says wrap after three — and was killed by the orchestrator's
1500s ceiling before writing any report. Run A's phone stopped after ~10
minutes of silence, probed once via the couch glance ("Partner, are you
okay? Last read shows 11 deaths"), and wrote a full report. The stop rule
works when followed; run B's phone report is the cost of not following it.

## Findings

1. **The jump verdict changes seat reasoning, live** *(Fact — the arc's
   question, answered)*. A seat derived an aim (630) from `lastStoodAt`
   data, tested it, and received `tookOff: true` with `pressedAt` and
   `apexY`. Pilot 4's blocker — attempted and absent jumps returning
   identical replies — did not reproduce. The remaining gap is no longer
   feedback on the jump; it is feedback on the *release* (finding 3).

2. **The arm-ordering protocol fix eliminated the deadlock class** *(Fact)*.
   15/15 arms fired, zero timeouts, zero double-blocked stalls, against
   pilot 4's 3 timeouts and two ~90s stalls. Cost: nothing observed. The
   prompts' "ask and arm in the same turn" and the phone's hold-until-armed
   rule were both followed by three of four seats without drift (run B's
   phone even initiated it).

3. **A death that completes between moves is invisible, and the jump death
   is in that class** *(Fact — new)*. Three of run A's eleven deaths — a
   `reached-x` that ended mid-fall, a freeze that lapsed while parked, and
   the pilot's one real jump — produced no `diedAt`/`lastStoodAt` anywhere:
   the `/move` had already returned, and `/state`'s `lastDeath` correctly
   goes stale-guarded rather than lying. The freshness rule works; the data
   is simply absent, and it is absent precisely at moves that end at their
   target. *(Recommendation — harness)* The driver should keep capturing
   deaths while *no* move is running — a light idle sampler updating
   `lastDeath`, so the next `/state` can answer "where" instead of only
   "again". It observes; it does not act; the honesty split is untouched.

4. **A stale platform makes every platform arm an instant, unprotected
   "go"** *(Fact — pilot 4's single case, now systematic)*. Five stale-
   platform arms, five instant fires (60–64ms), five deaths. With platforms
   persisting indefinitely, the platform trigger is permanently true after
   the run's first cast, and seats kept using it as though it still meant
   "when the cast lands". *(Recommendation)* The trigger-life backlog item
   covers the diagnosis side (`freezeMsLeft`, and the platform equivalent);
   the prompt side could teach "check `platformCount` before arming on
   platform — if one is standing, the arm fires immediately and buys you
   nothing", the same way it teaches the freeze protocol now.

5. **Better coordination shortened the runs** *(Fact — harness/pi, not the
   game)*. Turn cadence rose ~40–70% over pilot 4 once the stalls were
   gone, and both laptops hit the 250k input-tokens/min cap sooner (3m27s,
   1m54s). Live co-op is now bounded by the free tier, not by the pair's
   play. The token-rate-429 retryability item (BACKLOG Active — referenced
   by title; numbers shift) is the unblock; until then, pilots should
   space runs by several minutes and expect ~2–4 minutes of play.

6. **The support seat's feedback ask survives with the glance answering
   part of it** *(Fact — consistent with pilot 4's finding 6)*. Run A's
   phone used `worldLastDeath` correctly and immediately ("Caught the death
   — looks like you were near 328", 03:17:47, quoting the death site, not
   the respawn point), and its report still asks for the same thing on the
   phone screen itself: platform placement and jump-failure feedback that a
   pair in separate rooms would not have. The game-UI finding stands; the
   glance is a harness affordance and was labeled as such.

## Honesty caveats

- **Armed moves are driver-reflex.** Every patrol-band crossing in both
  runs rode an armed move; the seats chose maneuvers and triggers, the
  driver held and fired them. The 15/15 figure in finding 2 is armed-move
  progress and should be read with that discount.
- **The driver solved every puzzle.** Phone seats chose which power and
  when; solve times (freeze ~0.67s, platform ~4.85s, illuminate ~0.2s) are
  driver mechanics, consistent with pilots 3–4's bands.
- **The couch glance is a harness affordance, not a game feature.** Both
  phone-seat death citations came from `worldLastDeath`; the phone screen
  shows none of it.
- **Both runs are truncated; neither laptop report was written** — the 429
  killed both seats before that step, so laptop-side claims come from their
  notes files, intercom traffic, and the session transcripts. Run B's phone
  report was never written (killed at the orchestrator ceiling mid-wait);
  run A's phone report exists and is quoted above.
- **Death counts are not a quality metric** across runs of different
  lengths and interventions.
- The jump analysis rests on one trial. `diedAt`'s known biases (one-poll
  lag, fall-registration offset) apply to every table row;
  `reports/video/20260813-221712/laptop.webm` is the way to falsify the
  overflight reading.
